Hybrid thresholds after WoodelfHD v2

hybrid_woodelf hands a model to sparse WOODELF above depth 12 (background) and depth 10 (path dependent). Those thresholds were tuned in May against WoodelfHD v1. With v2 and the NumPy decision patterns of PR #22, WoodelfHD stays faster far past both, and how far depends on the metric and on the background size. This page has the measurements, a proposed rule scored against them, the CPU check of the neighbor leaf trick cutoff, and the GPU notebook for the trick.

CPU: AMD Ryzen 7 5700U, 8 cores, 15 W laptop · 38 GBWindows 11 · Python 3.12.7 · NumPy 2.4.6 · LightGBM 4.6.0 · XGBoost 3.0.5GPU: none here, Colab notebook ready

Recommendations

Background values (SV, BV)
depth > 12 depth > 11 + log₂ m
m is the number of background rows. WoodelfHD then runs up to depth 17 at m = 100, 20 at m = 1,000 and 24 at m = 10,000, and never above 24. Best rule measured, but not universal: near-full XGBoost trees and data with few distinct rows (KDD-99) favour sparse WOODELF earlier.
Background interaction values (SIV, BIV)
depth > 12 depth > 24
WoodelfHD v2 wins at every depth up to 24, by 3.54× to 207×. Above 24 its O(2D) vectors per leaf take gigabytes, so sparse WOODELF stays as the memory guard. CII metrics, still on WoodelfHD v1: depth > 7 + log₂ m, at most 18.
Path dependent, per metric
depth > 10 SV 16 · SIV 22 · BV 10
Per metric, with depth measured as the longest decision pattern. Shapley values cross at about 16, interaction values deeper, and Banzhaf values keep today's 10.
Neighbor leaf trick, CPU cutoff
D ≤ 12, n + m ≥ 10·2D always on (CPU)
On CPU the trick costs at most 17% where it loses and saves up to 1.67× where 0.5.0 turns it off. Keep the 0.5.0 cutoff for GPU=True until the Colab numbers are in.
Neighbor leaf trick, GPU
needs a CUDA GPU
Not timed here: this laptop has no NVIDIA GPU. The Colab notebook is ready and checked; see section 4.

Implemented after this benchmark, which timed woodelf 0.5.0 before the change. hybrid_woodelf passes the background size, and before it picks sparse WOODELF it computes the longest decision pattern (get_max_unique_features_in_path in woodelf/core/utils.py, about 1 µs per leaf), because min(max_depth, features) only bounds it. Task 1 · threshold 1

Background SHAP: WoodelfHD or sparse WOODELF

Both sides of the threshold are timed as hybrid_woodelf calls them: woodelf_for_high_depth (with its own trick cutoff) and woodelf_sparse, on the same parsed model and the same trees. The models are IEEE-CIS with the paper notebooks' LightGBM parameters (2,024 leaves at most, at least 500 rows per leaf) at max_depth 12 to 24, 10 trees, and 10,000 consumers. The charts plot sparse time ÷ WoodelfHD time on a log scale: above 1, WoodelfHD is faster.

  • Shapley values, small background. With m = 100, WoodelfHD stays ahead up to depth 17 (1.04×) and sparse WOODELF wins from 18 (0.76×, then 0.31× at 20 and 0.10× at 24).
  • Every 10× more background rows moves the crossover about 3.3 depths deeper. With m = 1,000 WoodelfHD leads up to about 21 (1.42× at 20, 0.76× at 22). With m = 10,000 it still leads at 24 (1.50×). Per leaf, sparse WOODELF pays for every pair of distinct consumer and background patterns, and their number grows with m. WoodelfHD pays O(D·2D) whatever m is. The crossover sits where 2D matches that pair count, so it moves by log₂ 10 ≈ 3.3 depths per decade of m. That is the form of the proposed rule.
  • Interaction values never cross up to depth 24. WoodelfHD v2 builds every pair from three shared s vectors, while sparse WOODELF's order-2 CII path pays for every pair and every pattern pair: 3.54× at m = 100 and depth 24, 50× at m = 1,000 and depth 18.
  • Today's threshold (12) is wrong for 60 of the 100 clean background configs on this page, and costs up to 207×.

These models were not used to choose the rule's offsets, so they are its out-of-sample check. Two of them break it, for reasons that point to a better rule:

  • Deep LightGBM (10,000 leaves) and HIGGS follow it. Deep LightGBM crosses at about 17 with m = 100 (0.93× at 16, 0.54× at 18) and still favours WoodelfHD at 22 with m = 10,000 (1.33×). HIGGS favours WoodelfHD at every depth: its paths hold at most 13 distinct features even at max_depth 18, so its patterns stay short. That is why the proposal measures depth as the longest decision pattern rather than min(max_depth, features).
  • XGBoost depth-wise, small background: sparse wins earlier. With m = 100 it wins already at 12 (0.92×) and by 16 it is ahead (0.44×), where the rule still picks WoodelfHD. These trees are nearly full (711 leaves at depth 12 against 172 for the paper's LightGBM), so many leaves have long patterns and WoodelfHD's Σ 2pattern length grows fast. With m = 10,000 WoodelfHD still wins (2.20× at 18).
  • KDD-99: sparse wins from 15 even with m = 100,000 (0.77× at 15, 0.58× at 20). The data has very few distinct rows, so the number of distinct background patterns stays small however large m is. The rule's log₂ m stands in for that number, and here it overstates it.

The better signal is the one the cost model names: Σ 2pattern length over the leaves (from the tree, free) against the number of distinct consumer and background patterns (from the data, which a small sample would estimate). That is a bigger change than a threshold, so it is a follow-up. The m-aware rule is still the best of the rules measured here, on every model together.

Task 1 · threshold 2

Path-dependent SHAP: WoodelfHD or Linear TreeSHAP

The same models, no background. The sparse side is woodelf_sparse with no background data, which runs the recursive Linear TreeSHAP. Above 1, WoodelfHD is faster.

  • Shapley values cross at about 14 to 16. With n = 1,000: 1.03× at 14, 0.84× at 15. With n = 10,000: 1.14× at 15, 0.99× at 16. With n = 100,000: 1.16× at 16. The ratios stay close to 1 across that band, so one threshold (16) costs little either way. Over every model and dataset it is never more than 1.6× off.
  • Interaction values favour WoodelfHD much longer: 6.08× at 12, 4.92× at 17 and 2.45× at 22, the deepest measured. Linear TreeSHAP's interaction values run one Shapley pass per feature, so its cost grows with an extra factor of the depth. The proposal uses 22, the deepest depth measured.
  • Banzhaf values favour Linear TreeSHAP early: its Banzhaf path is a closed-form product with no polynomial multiplication, 0.78× at 12. Here n decides: with n = 1,000 Linear TreeSHAP already wins at depth 8 (0.76×), with n = 100,000 WoodelfHD still wins at 11 (1.26×). Every threshold from 8 to 11 stays within 1.26× to 1.40× of the best, and today's 10 has the lowest total time, so Banzhaf keeps 10.

Task 1 · threshold 3

The neighbor leaf trick cutoff on CPU

woodelf_for_high_depth turns the trick off when max_depth > 12 or n + m < 10 · 2max_depth. Here WoodelfHD runs with the trick forced on and forced off (the cutoff bypassed, the same per-tree code). Cells are seconds without the trick ÷ seconds with it, so above 1 the trick helps. A small on marks the cells where 0.5.0 uses the trick.

trick ≥ 5% fasterwithin 5%trick ≥ 5% slower
  • With 10,000 rows or more, the trick helps at every depth measured, 6 to 16. That includes the cells 0.5.0 turns off: depth 12 with n = m = 10,000 (1.25× for SIV), depth 15 (1.67× for SIV, 1.23× for SV with 100,000 rows) and KDD-99 at depth 15 (1.24×).
  • With 1,000 rows it is a wash, and it loses only for background Shapley values at depth 12 and deeper, by 5% to 17% (0.86× at depth 15).
  • Few features change little. On California Housing (8 features, so patterns of at most 8 bits at any depth) the trick stays within 0.95× to 1.27×. Using the effective depth in the cutoff fixes its two misses there, but leaves the larger misses elsewhere.
  • Why: v2 folds the neighbor leaf into the s vectors only when n > 2D, and otherwise adds the two leaves one after the other. Either way the right leaf's decision patterns and bincount are skipped, and that saving does not depend on the depth.

Task 2

The neighbor leaf trick on a real GPU

not run yet: needs a CUDA GPU

This laptop has no NVIDIA GPU (a Ryzen 7 5700U with integrated Radeon graphics), and CuPy needs CUDA, so the GPU timings are left to Colab, on the same T4 High-RAM setup as the paper rerun notebooks. The notebook is ready: treebranchmarks/colab/woodelf_neighbor_trick_gpu.ipynb.

What it times
woodelf_for_high_depth of woodelf 0.5.0 (the PyPI wheel, byte-identical to main's woodelf/) with the trick forced on and forced off. It bypasses the cutoff by calling woodelf_for_high_depth_single_tree directly: the same p2s, the same per-tree code, the same mirroring.
Grid
IEEE-CIS, KDD-99, HIGGS and California Housing · BG SHAP, BG SHAP IV, PD SHAP, PD SHAP IV · depths 6, 9, 12, 15, 18 · 1k, 10k, 100k rows and the full data. It uses the first trees of the notebooks' 100-tree models: 10 trees in general, and 1 to 3 trees for the deep HIGGS models.
Before any timing
A correctness cell compares trick on and off, on GPU and on CPU, at depths 6 and 12 (both the full s vector and the consumers-only branches). The Shapley values must also add up to the predictions.
Send back
The woodelfhd_rerun/trick_gpu_*.csv files, one per Colab machine. The last cell prints the speedup of the trick per cell, the 0.5.0 cutoff's choice, and the total time of never, always, the cutoff, and the best choice per run.

Checked here first

  • Every code cell ran end to end with NumPy standing in for CuPy, so the GPU=True branches of woodelf ran. It used a small fake Drive folder with the paper's file names and columns, and logging, resuming and the summary tables all worked. The stand-in timings mean nothing.
  • The correctness cell passed: trick on and off, "GPU" and CPU give the same values for BG and PD, Shapley values and interaction values.
  • The notebook reuses the rerun notebooks' PARAMS, load_dataset and get_model unchanged, so it shares their model files, and it slices the first trees of the saved 100-tree models rather than retraining.

What to expect, and what would change the cutoff

For a pair of sibling leaves, the trick skips the right leaf's decision patterns and bincount. With many consumers (n > 2D) it also folds the right leaf into the s vectors, so the consumers are gathered once per feature instead of twice. With few consumers it adds the two leaves one after the other, which saves only the patterns. It adds a second shared-s-vector computation and a neighbor_vector pass (about eight small kernels) per pair. On a GPU, gathers over n are cheap and every kernel launch has a fixed cost, so the trick should pay only when n is large. If the CSVs show that the trick loses at sizes where 0.5.0 turns it on, the fix is a GPU-specific cutoff (for example a larger row multiple when GPU=True).

How it was measured

  • Code. git archive v0.5.0 woodelf (= main, 483d72c), first on sys.path, so neither the stale 0.4.6 install in .venv nor the working tree (edited by another session at the time) could leak in.
  • Data. The treebranchmarks cache: IEEE-CIS (590,540 × 397, 80/20 split), KDD-99 (4.9M × 121, a 2M train sample), HIGGS (11M × 28: the first 2M rows train, the last 1M test), California Housing (the paper's split). Consumers are the first n test rows, the background m train rows sampled with seed 0.
  • Models. LightGBM with the paper notebooks' parameters at max_depth D; LightGBM deep (10,000 leaves, 20 rows per leaf); XGBoost depth-wise (hist, min_child_weight 1). The model is parsed once, outside the timing.
  • Timing. Both methods run on the same trees, interleaved A B, B A, best of two under 20 seconds. When the slower method would pass the budget (30 s for the crossover plans, 20 s for the rest) on all trees, an evenly spaced subset of k trees is timed (calibrated on the middle tree). Ratios use those trees, and seconds are scaled to all trees. The first boosting tree is not used alone: it was much cheaper than average for sparse WOODELF.
  • Correctness. Every config compares WoodelfHD's values with the other method's. The largest difference, relative to the largest value, was 5e-08 in background mode and 7e-06 in path-dependent mode.
  • A shared laptop. Another Claude session ran CPU benchmarks on this machine at the same time. Before each config the runner waited while other Python processes kept 2 or more cores busy, and it recorded their load during the run. The 26 rows that ran next to more than 1.5 busy cores were timed again on a quiet machine, and those second timings are the ones shown. One busy core did not move the ratios: the same config gave 0.233 with and without it. Absolute seconds on this 15 W laptop vary by up to 2× with its thermal state, so read the ratios, not the seconds.
  • The old thresholds, for comparison. The same script on v0.4.8, the last release before WoodelfHD v2, shows that 12 and 10 were right for it: sparse WOODELF already won at depth 12 with a 100-row background and in path-dependent mode. In 0.5.0 WoodelfHD got about 3× faster at depth 12 and 11× to 20× faster at depth 15, while sparse WOODELF barely moved. That is what moved the crossovers.

Side findings

  • The 0.5.0 wheel on PyPI ships woodelf/woodelfhd_paper.py. The file is untracked in git, so the wheel was built from a working tree that had it. It imports woodelf.cube_metric, woodelf.decision_trees_ensemble and woodelf.parse_models, paths that no longer exist, so importing it fails. Nothing imports it, so it is harmless. Building the next release from a clean checkout keeps it out. Apart from this file, the wheel's woodelf/ is identical to git archive v0.5.0.
  • The trick cutoff looks at max_depth, not at the pattern length. One line above the cutoff, woodelf_for_high_depth computes effective_max_decision_pattern_length = min(model_obj.max_depth, len(consumer_data.keys())), but the cutoff tests model_obj.max_depth > 12 and n + m < 10 * 2**model_obj.max_depth. On data with few features the patterns stay short at any depth: California Housing has 8 features, so a depth-15 model has patterns of at most 8 bits, yet the trick is off. The California rows in section 3 test this.
